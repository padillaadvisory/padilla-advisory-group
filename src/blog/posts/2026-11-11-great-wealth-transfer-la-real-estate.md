You've probably seen the headline number. A few years ago, researchers put it at $84 trillion: the amount older Americans are expected to pass to the next generation. The estimate has grown since. Cerulli Associates now projects nearly $124 trillion changing hands through 2048, with about $79 trillion of that coming from baby boomers and older Americans, going to younger generations and charities.

Those are national numbers about all kinds of assets. But if you own a long-held home in Los Angeles, it's reasonable to ask what any of it means for you.

## Why this matters to a homeowner

For most families, the house is the largest single asset in the estate. In Los Angeles, a home bought in the 1980s or 1990s may now be worth many times its purchase price. When that home is inherited, someone has to decide what to do with it, usually under time pressure and often with several heirs who need to agree.

That decision happens in a lot of homes at once. The wealth transfer is a trend, but for any one family it shows up as a very specific question: sell, keep, rent, or buy out a sibling.

## What it's doing to the market

You can see a few effects in how homes come to market:

- **More estate and heir-driven sales.** These listings tend to be well-located, longtime-owned homes that have not been updated, which shapes how buyers and investors price them.
- **Heirs who live elsewhere.** Many heirs are Gen X and millennials who already own a home or live out of state, so they're more likely to sell than move in.
- **A bigger role for cash and investor buyers.** Condition-heavy estate properties attract buyers who can close quickly and take on a project.

None of this means prices go up or down in any particular way. It means a meaningful share of inventory is being shaped by life events instead of by choice.

## Where owners go wrong

The families who lose the most are rarely the ones who made a bad choice. They are the ones who made no choice and let the timing be set for them. Three patterns come up again and again:

- The home's ownership and documents aren't in order, so heirs face probate or a stalled sale.
- Siblings disagree on whether to sell, and the house sits while taxes, insurance, and upkeep accumulate.
- The tax basis and Prop 19 consequences for heirs weren't considered, which can change what keeping the house actually costs.

California's Prop 19 limits the property tax benefit that passes to children when a parent's home is inherited, particularly if the child doesn't use it as a primary residence. A CPA or attorney should walk you through how that applies to your family.

## If you're the one who owns the house

A few moves now cost little and help a lot later:

1. Get the legal structure right: a will, trust, or other plan reviewed by an attorney, and clear beneficiary designations.
2. Tell the family what the plan is, so there's no surprise at the worst possible moment.
3. Get a current value on the house, and compare keeping, selling now, and selling later.
4. Ask a CPA how a sale now versus a transfer at death changes the tax picture for you and your heirs.

## If you're an heir

Slow down on the first decision. Find out who has the legal authority, get a valuation, understand carrying costs, and compare the net result of selling versus keeping before anyone commits.

**The takeaway:** the wealth transfer is huge in aggregate and personal in practice. For a homeowner, the useful question is not whether it's happening but whether your family will decide on its own timeline or have one chosen for it.
