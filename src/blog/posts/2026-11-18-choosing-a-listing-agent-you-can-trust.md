If you've owned your home for 25 or 30 years, the last time you hired a listing agent may have been the last time you sold, which could be never. And if you're handling a parent's house, an estate, or a sale in the middle of a divorce, you're hiring someone to handle what may be the largest single asset in the family, often while you're stretched thin.

The worry is reasonable: How do I know this person is working for me and not just for a fast commission? Here's how to answer that with facts instead of a gut feeling.

## 1. Verify the License Before You Take the Meeting

In California, anyone who lists your home for a fee must hold an active real estate license from the Department of Real Estate (DRE). The DRE's free public license lookup at dre.ca.gov shows whether a license is active, which brokerage the agent works under, and whether there's any public disciplinary history.

This takes two minutes. Do it for every agent you interview. If the name doesn't come up, or there's a disciplinary record you weren't told about, you have your answer.

## 2. Ask for Numbers, Not Adjectives

Every agent will tell you they're "experienced," "local," and "a top producer." None of that helps you decide. Ask for specifics:

- How many homes have you personally listed and sold in the last 12–24 months, and how many were in this area or price range?
- What was your average list-to-sale price ratio, and how long did those homes take to sell?
- How many of your listings expired or were withdrawn without selling?
- Can you show me two or three recent listings similar to mine, with what they listed for and what they actually sold for?

The last question matters most. An agent who can walk you through the gap between list price and sale price on real homes understands pricing. An agent who only talks about how high they can list your home is often setting you up for price cuts later.

## 3. Watch How They Arrive at a Price

The most common way sellers get hurt isn't fraud. It's an agent who "buys the listing" by quoting a price higher than the market will support, knowing you'll pick the agent with the biggest number. The home sits, the price drops in stages, and buyers start wondering what's wrong with it.

A trustworthy agent brings a comparative market analysis built from recent closed sales, explains which comps they weighted and why, and gives you a range with reasoning behind it. If three agents give you similar ranges and one comes in 15% higher with no data to back it, be skeptical of the outlier, not grateful for it.

## 4. Read the Listing Agreement Like It's a Contract (Because It Is)

The listing agreement governs your entire relationship. Before you sign, make sure you understand:

- **The term.** California exclusive listings must have a definite end date. Shorter terms, or a clear cancellation provision, keep the agent accountable.
- **The commission.** Commissions are negotiable and always have been. Since the 2024 national settlement involving the National Association of REALTORS®, offers of compensation to buyer's agents are no longer posted on the MLS, so ask exactly how buyer-agent compensation will be handled, what you're agreeing to pay, and when.
- **Who represents whom.** California allows an agent or brokerage to represent both buyer and seller (dual agency) only with written consent from both. Ask directly whether the agent intends to sell your home to their own buyer and how they'd handle that conflict.
- **What's included.** Photography, staging consultation, pre-sale inspections, marketing spend. Get it in writing, not in a handshake.

If an agent rushes you through the paperwork or gets defensive about questions, that tells you how they'll handle the rest of the sale.

## 5. Make Sure They Understand Your Kind of Sale

A life-transition sale is not the same as a standard move-up sale. Depending on your situation, the agent you hire may need to coordinate with:

- A probate attorney and the court timeline, if the home is in an estate.
- Two spouses with separate attorneys, possibly not speaking to each other, in a divorce.
- Siblings spread across the country who all have a say in an inherited home.
- A parent's move date into assisted living, where the sale has to fund the next step.

Ask how many sales like yours they've handled and what went wrong on one of them. Designations such as the Seniors Real Estate Specialist (SRES), awarded by the National Association of REALTORS® after specialized coursework, show an agent has trained for older sellers' needs. Training isn't a substitute for track record, but it's a useful signal.

## 6. Know the Red Flags

Any one of these is reason to keep interviewing:

- Pressure to sign at the first meeting.
- A price far above every other agent's, with no comps to support it.
- Vague answers about who they represent or whether they'll bring their own buyer.
- Suggesting you sell to them, or to an investor they know, without showing you what the open market would bring.
- Communicating only with one family member when several have authority over the sale.

If you're helping an aging parent sell, pay attention to that last one. A good agent includes the right people and documents decisions, which protects everyone.

## The Takeaway

Trust isn't something an agent asks for. It's something you verify: a clean license, real numbers on recent sales, a price backed by data, a contract you fully understand, and experience with sales like yours. Interview at least two or three agents, ask every one of them the same questions, and compare the answers side by side. The right agent will welcome the scrutiny, because it's the same discipline they should be bringing to the sale of your home.
