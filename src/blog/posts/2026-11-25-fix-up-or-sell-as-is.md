You walk through the house with fresh eyes and see what a buyer will see: the 1990s kitchen, the worn carpet, the bathroom that needs a new vanity, the garage full of 30 years of life. The question comes fast. Do I have to fix all of this before I sell? And if I'm selling a parent's house from across town or across the country, who is going to manage a remodel?

Here's the short answer: you almost never have to renovate. But "do nothing" isn't always the best-paying option either. The right move depends on the number that actually matters, which is what you walk away with.

## The Number That Matters Is Your Net, Not Your Price

Sellers tend to fixate on the sale price. The better question is: after repairs, carrying costs, and time, which path leaves you with the most money and the least strain?

A $60,000 kitchen remodel that adds $40,000 to the price is a loss, before you count the three months of property taxes, insurance, and utilities while the work gets done. A $3,000 weekend of paint, cleaning, and minor repairs that helps the home sell faster and closer to asking is often the best return in the whole process.

## What "As-Is" Really Means in California

Selling as-is means you aren't agreeing to make repairs. It does not mean you can skip disclosures.

California sellers generally still have to complete the required disclosure forms, including the Transfer Disclosure Statement, and share known material facts about the property, unless a specific exemption applies (some probate and trust sales, for example, have different rules). Buyers will still inspect, and they'll still negotiate based on what they find.

That's why many sellers order a **pre-listing inspection**. You learn about the roof, the sewer line, or the electrical panel before a buyer does, so you can price for it, fix it, or disclose it upfront instead of renegotiating in the middle of escrow.

## Where the Money Comes Back (and Where It Doesn't)

Zonda's 2025 Cost vs. Value report tracks how much of a project's cost shows up in resale value. In the Los Angeles and Pacific numbers, the pattern is clear:

- **Garage door replacement** costs roughly $4,500 and recoups well over 200% of its cost in resale value.
- **A new steel entry door** costs roughly $2,500 and recoups about twice its cost.
- **A minor, midrange kitchen refresh** (cabinet fronts, counters, appliances, fixtures) recoups somewhere around 113% to 129%.
- **A major midrange kitchen remodel** costs over $80,000 and recoups only about half of that.
- **A midrange bathroom remodel** recoups roughly 80% to 90%.

The takeaway: small, visible, first-impression projects tend to pay. Big renovations usually don't, at least not when you're the one selling.

Staging belongs in the "small and visible" category. In the National Association of REALTORS® 2025 Profile of Home Staging, 49% of sellers' agents said staging reduced time on market, and 29% said it increased the dollar value of offers by 1% to 10%. The median cost was about $1,500 when a staging service was used. The rooms staged most often were the living room, the primary bedroom, the dining room, and the kitchen.

## Three Paths, and Who Each One Fits

**Sell as-is, cleaned out.** Best when the home needs major work (foundation, roof, full systems), when the seller can't manage a project, or when an estate needs a faster, simpler sale. Expect investor and contractor buyers, and price it accordingly.

**Prep and present.** Clear it out, deep clean, paint, fix the obvious items, handle a few high-return projects, and stage. This is the right path for most long-held homes. It reaches the widest pool of buyers without a renovation budget.

**Renovate before listing.** Rarely the right call. It makes sense mainly when the home is badly out of step with the neighborhood and you have the time, the cash, and the stomach for contractors. Even then, run the numbers first.

## How to Decide in an Afternoon

Before spending a dollar, get three things in writing:

1. A pre-listing inspection, so you know what you're working with.
2. A pricing opinion for the home in two conditions: as-is, and prepped and staged.
3. Real bids for the prep work, not guesses.

Subtract the prep cost from the difference between those two prices. If the gap is meaningful, prep. If it's thin, or if the time and stress would land on someone who's already carrying too much, as-is is a perfectly sound decision. ## The Takeaway

You don't have to fix everything, and you shouldn't. Skip the big remodel, disclose honestly, and spend only where the numbers show a return: a clean, cleared-out home, fresh paint, small visible upgrades, and staging. Decide based on your net and your capacity, not on the fear of what a buyer might think of the kitchen.
