The house is usually the biggest asset in a divorce and the one with the most emotion attached. Before you list it, refinance it, or fight over it, there are a handful of decisions that shape everything that follows. Get them in the right order and the sale tends to go smoothly. Get them backwards and you can lose real money to taxes, carrying costs, or a rushed price.

## Decide first: sell, buy out, or defer

California is a community property state, so the marital home is generally treated as owned equally, regardless of whose name is on the title or who paid the mortgage. That leaves three basic paths:

- **Sell and split the net proceeds.** After the mortgage and closing costs, what's left is divided. On an $800,000 home with a $400,000 loan, expect roughly $350,000 to split, or about $175,000 each, once costs come out.
- **Buyout.** One spouse keeps the house and pays the other their share of the equity, usually by refinancing into their own name. The spouse keeping the home has to qualify for the loan alone, which is the step that sinks many buyouts at today's rates.
- **Deferred sale.** Where there are minor children, a court may allow the custodial parent to stay in the home until a set date, such as when the youngest turns 18, with the sale and equity split postponed.

If one spouse can't realistically qualify to refinance, the real choice is often sell now or sell later, not sell versus keep.

## Agree on the value before you agree on anything else

Most disputes in a home sale during divorce come back to one number. Courts generally expect a certified appraisal, not an online estimate, and an appraisal typically costs a few hundred dollars. If you disagree, each side can hire an appraiser, or the court can appoint a neutral one.

A pricing opinion from a listing agent is a different tool. It tells you what the market will likely pay, in what time frame, and what condition-related adjustments matter, which is what you need to set a list price. Use both: the appraisal for the legal split, the market analysis for the sale strategy. Ask for both spouses to be copied on everything so no one feels the number was set behind their back.

## Understand the tax timing

The federal home sale exclusion lets a single owner exclude up to $250,000 of gain on a primary residence and a married couple filing jointly up to $500,000, provided the ownership and use tests are met (generally two of the last five years). The date the divorce becomes final matters:

- If you're still legally married at year-end, you're treated as married for the full year, and may be able to claim the larger joint exclusion.
- If the divorce is final by year-end, each spouse is treated as single and can exclude up to $250,000 of their own share.

There's a second trap: if one spouse moves out and the sale drags on, that spouse can eventually fail the two-year use test. A divorce agreement can address this by allowing the other spouse to stay in the home for a defined period, but it generally has to be negotiated before the divorce is final. Your CPA and attorney should settle this before the listing agreement is signed.

## Plan for the mortgage and the separate-property question

A divorce decree doesn't remove a spouse from the loan. The lender has to agree to a refinance or assumption, so a spouse can stay legally liable on a house they no longer own or live in.

Also flag any separate-property money that went into the house, such as an inheritance used for the down payment. California law may allow reimbursement of those contributions before the remaining equity is split, but it takes documentation. Gather the paper trail early, because tracing the funds after the sale closes is much harder.

## Respect the calendar

California imposes a mandatory six-month waiting period from service of the divorce papers before a judgment can be entered. Uncontested cases often finalize in about six to eight months, while contested property disputes can run a year or two. A home is expensive to carry in the meantime: mortgage, taxes, insurance, and upkeep keep running whether or not anyone agrees on a listing price.

In practice, that argues for deciding the property early. A neutral, well-prepared sale is often worth more than a drawn-out fight over the same house.

**The takeaway:** the right order is value, then path, then taxes, then listing. Settle what the house is worth, decide whether it's a sale, a buyout, or a deferral, have a CPA check the tax timing, and only then go to market. Couples who sequence it that way usually keep more of the equity.
