You were named executor, or the family asked you to step in. There is a house, a stack of paperwork, and a question you'd rather not get wrong: how do I sell this property legally, without leaving the estate exposed and without it dragging on for years?

The short answer is that a California probate sale follows a set order of steps, and the single biggest lever on your timeline is one decision made early: whether the estate has court-approved authority to sell without a confirmation hearing.

## Step 1: Get your legal authority first

You cannot list a probate house on the strength of a will alone. The court must appoint you and issue **Letters Testamentary** (if there is a will) or **Letters of Administration** (if there isn't). Until you hold Letters, you can't sign a listing agreement or accept an offer.

Expect this to take weeks, not days. Everything downstream waits on it, so file the petition as soon as you've spoken with a probate attorney.

One check worth making before you go further: California has simplified procedures for some smaller estates and for a decedent's primary residence under a value cap (currently $750,000 under the state's 2024 law). If the home qualifies, you may skip full probate. Whether it does depends on facts a probate attorney needs to confirm, including how the home was titled.

## Step 2: Decide whether you'll sell with or without court confirmation

This is the fork in the road.

- **Independent Administration of Estates Act (IAEA) authority.** If the court grants full authority, you can sell the house much like a normal sale, after giving heirs a Notice of Proposed Action. Heirs have 15 days to object, or they can sign waivers to move faster.
- **Court-confirmed sale.** If you don't have that authority, or the court limits it, the accepted offer must be confirmed by a judge at a hearing. These are typically set 20 to 40 days after the petition is filed.

Full IAEA authority is faster and more predictable for buyers. Court confirmation is slower and opens the door to overbidding, but it is a perfectly normal path for many Los Angeles estates.

## Step 3: Get a date-of-death appraisal

A probate appraiser sets the value, and it matters twice. It anchors your pricing, and in a court-confirmed sale it sets the floor: the court generally won't confirm an offer below 90% of the appraised value.

It also matters for taxes. Inherited property generally receives a stepped-up tax basis to its value at the date of death, which can sharply reduce capital gains if the house sells soon after. Your CPA should confirm how this applies to your estate.

## Step 4: Prep, price, and list with a probate-savvy agent

Probate listings carry their own required addenda and advisories, and buyers' agents know to read them. A few practical points:

- The house is often dated, deferred in maintenance, or full of belongings. Decide early what gets cleared, what gets repaired, and what gets sold as-is. Selling as-is is common, but price it honestly against the condition.
- Pricing in a probate sale is not a guess. Your appraisal, recent comparable sales, and the cost of holding the property (taxes, insurance, utilities, upkeep) all belong in the conversation.
- Agent listing terms in probate are time-limited. An initial exclusive period is commonly up to 90 days, with court approval needed for extensions.

This is where a local agent earns their keep. Westside, Hollywood Hills, and Valley homes behave very differently, and a price that works in Studio City can stall in a canyon neighborhood.

## Step 5: Understand what overbidding does to your timeline

In a court-confirmed sale, your buyer's accepted offer is not final until the hearing. Other buyers can show up in court and bid higher. To open overbidding, a new bid generally must beat the original by at least 5% plus $500.

For the estate, that can mean a better price. For the original buyer, it's a risk, and some will walk away from the uncertainty. Good listing strategy accounts for both. If you're expecting a competitive, well-priced house, confirmation can work in the estate's favor.

## Step 6: Close, pay the bills, and distribute

After the sale closes, proceeds go into the estate account. You then pay creditors, taxes, and administration costs before making distributions to heirs. California sets statutory fees for both the attorney and the personal representative based on the gross value of the estate, which is why a house sale can have such a large effect on total costs. Your attorney can show you exactly how those fees work out on your estate.

## What the whole timeline looks like

A California probate commonly runs 12 to 18 months from filing to final distribution, and contested estates or those with real estate complications can run longer. The house itself can often be sold well before the estate closes, especially with IAEA authority. The delays that cost money are usually avoidable ones: waiting to file, neglecting vacant-home insurance, letting a property sit, or pricing too high and burning through the listing period.

**The takeaway:** your two biggest early decisions are getting Letters issued quickly and finding out what level of sale authority the court will give you. Make those two moves first, bring in a probate attorney and a probate-experienced agent together, and the sale becomes a sequence of manageable steps instead of an open-ended problem.
