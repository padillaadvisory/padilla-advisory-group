Most of your wealth is in the house. Your monthly income is tighter than you'd like. And you have two very different ways to turn that equity into money: borrow against it with a reverse mortgage and stay, or sell and move.

Both can be the right answer. Both can be expensive mistakes. The difference comes down to how long you plan to stay, what the house costs you to keep, and what you want to leave behind.

## How a Reverse Mortgage Actually Works

The most common type is the federally insured Home Equity Conversion Mortgage (HECM). The basics:

- You must be at least 62, and the home must be your primary residence.
- You must complete counseling with a HUD-approved counselor before the loan closes.
- You make no required monthly mortgage payments. Instead, interest and fees are added to the loan balance, which grows over time.
- You still owe property taxes, homeowner's insurance, and upkeep. Falling behind on those can make the loan come due early.
- The loan generally comes due when you sell, move out, or pass away. HECMs are non-recourse: you or your heirs won't owe more than the home is worth when it's sold.

For 2026, the maximum home value a HECM can be based on is $1,249,125, according to HUD. In much of Los Angeles, that cap matters. If your home is worth $2.5 million, a HECM only counts the first $1.25 million or so, and the amount you can actually borrow is a percentage of that, based on your age and current interest rates. Private "jumbo" reverse mortgages exist for higher-value homes, with their own terms.

## What It Costs

Reverse mortgages are not cheap money. A HECM typically includes an upfront FHA mortgage insurance premium of 2% of the home's value (up to the limit), an annual insurance premium on the balance, an origination fee, standard closing costs, and interest that compounds for as long as the loan is open.

That compounding is the part people underestimate. Over 10 or 15 years, a growing balance can consume a large share of the equity your family expected to inherit.

## When a Reverse Mortgage Can Make Sense

It tends to fit when most of these are true:

- You fully intend to stay in the home for many years.
- The house still works for you physically: stairs, size, upkeep.
- You can comfortably keep paying property taxes, insurance, and maintenance.
- Leaving the maximum amount of equity to heirs is not your top priority.
- You need income or a cushion, and selling would mean leaving a community you depend on.

## When Selling Is Usually the Better Move

Selling deserves a hard look if:

- The house is more than you need, or the upkeep is already wearing on you.
- You're likely to move within a few years anyway, for health, family, or simplicity.
- You want to preserve equity for your children instead of letting interest absorb it.
- Your home's value is well above the HECM limit, so a reverse mortgage would tap only part of your equity.

California also has rules that make selling less costly than many longtime owners assume. Under Proposition 19, homeowners 55 and older can generally transfer their property tax base to a replacement home anywhere in the state, up to three times. And the federal home sale exclusion lets qualifying sellers exclude up to $250,000 of gain ($500,000 for married couples filing jointly). For owners with a large gain, a CPA should run the numbers, but these two rules often change the math entirely.

## Run Both Scenarios Side by Side

Before you decide, get answers to five questions:

1. How much could I actually borrow, in a lump sum, as monthly payments, or as a line of credit, at today's rates?
2. What will the loan balance likely be in 5, 10, and 15 years?
3. What would I net from a sale today after commissions, closing costs, and taxes?
4. What would my next home cost, including property taxes under Prop 19?
5. What do I want my heirs to receive, and when?

A reverse mortgage counselor can answer the first two. A CPA can help with taxes. A seller-focused agent can give you a realistic net-proceeds estimate and replacement-home costs. Padilla Advisory Group is glad to prepare the selling side of that comparison so you can see both paths on one page.

## The Takeaway

A reverse mortgage is a tool for staying put. Selling is a tool for resetting. If the home still fits your life and you plan to stay for the long haul, a reverse mortgage can provide income without moving. If the home is too big, too costly, or likely to be sold within a few years anyway, selling usually preserves more of your equity and gives you more choices. Decide with both sets of numbers in front of you, not just one.
