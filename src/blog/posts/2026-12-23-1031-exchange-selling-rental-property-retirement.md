You bought the duplex in Silver Lake in 1994, or the fourplex your parents left you, or the little rental house you kept when you moved across town. It's been a good investment. It's also been 30 years of tenant calls and broken water heaters. You're ready to sell, but when you run the numbers, the capital gains bill makes you want to keep fixing water heaters forever.

That's the situation a 1031 exchange was built for.

## First, the Big Caveat: This Is for Investment Property, Not Your Home

A 1031 exchange applies to real property held for investment or for use in a business. That means rentals, multi-unit buildings, commercial property, and land. It does **not** apply to the house you live in.

If you're selling your primary residence, the tool you want is the Section 121 exclusion: up to $250,000 of gain tax-free if you're single, $500,000 if you're married filing jointly, as long as you've owned and lived in the home for two of the last five years.

Where it gets interesting is mixed-use situations: a home with a rental unit in back, a duplex where you live in one side, or a former residence you've been renting out. Those can qualify for **both** benefits, split between the portion you lived in and the portion you rented, under IRS guidance (Rev. Proc. 2005-14). That's a conversation for your CPA before you list, not after.

## How a 1031 Exchange Works

The concept is simple: sell one investment property, buy another of equal or greater value, and defer the capital gains tax (and depreciation recapture) instead of paying it now. The rules are strict:

- **A qualified intermediary holds the sale proceeds.** If the money touches your bank account, the exchange is blown. The intermediary must be in place before you close.
- **45 days to identify replacement property.** The clock starts the day your sale closes. You submit your list in writing.
- **180 days to close on the replacement, or your tax return due date if that comes first.** That's 180 days total from the sale, not 180 after the 45. If you sell late in the year, the April filing deadline can cut the window short unless you file an extension. Weekends and holidays count.
- **Reinvest everything to defer everything.** To defer all of the gain, buy property of equal or greater value and reinvest all of the net proceeds. Any cash you pull out, or any drop in mortgage debt you don't make up with new debt or added cash, is "boot," and boot is taxable up to the amount of your gain.

For sellers, the practical takeaway is that the 45-day window is short. If you don't have a plan for the replacement property before you list, you're negotiating your sale and shopping your purchase under the same deadline.

## Why Downsizers Use It: Equity Without the Landlord Job

Most retirees don't want a bigger building. They want to stop being a landlord without handing a big chunk of their equity to the IRS and Sacramento. The common moves:

### 1. Trade management-heavy for management-light

Sell the fourplex with four sets of tenants and buy a single-tenant net-lease property (think a pharmacy or quick-service restaurant on a long lease) where the tenant pays taxes, insurance, and most maintenance.

### 2. Exchange into a Delaware Statutory Trust (DST)

A DST lets you own a fractional interest in a large, professionally managed property, like an apartment community or medical building, and the IRS treats it as qualifying replacement property. It's fully passive. The trade-offs are real: DSTs are generally limited to accredited investors, they're illiquid (there's generally no public market, so plan on staying in until the sponsor sells the property), and they carry fees. They're securities sold through licensed financial professionals.

### 3. Split the proceeds

You can exchange part of the proceeds and take part in cash. You'll pay tax on the cash portion, but that can be the right answer if you need liquidity for a move, a care facility, or a gift to the kids.

### 4. Hold and let the step-up do the work

This one matters for estate planning. Under current federal law, when you pass away, your heirs generally receive the property at a "stepped-up" cost basis equal to its value at your death. A deferred gain that's been rolled forward through one or more exchanges can effectively disappear. For many families, exchanging into passive property and holding for life is the whole strategy.

## The California Wrinkle

California follows the federal 1031 rules, but it keeps an eye on you. If you sell a California property and exchange into property outside the state, California doesn't forget the deferred gain. You'll be required to file Form FTB 3840 every year until you eventually sell in a taxable transaction, at which point California collects its share of the gain it deferred, even if you live in Arizona by then. Selling and moving out of state doesn't reset the bill.

If you want to eventually move into the replacement property yourself, there's another rule: under federal law, you can't use the home sale exclusion on a property you got in a 1031 exchange if you sell it within five years of acquiring it. Even after that, the years it was a rental can reduce how much of the gain you can exclude.

## What This Means Before You List

The tax strategy should shape the sale, not the other way around. Before your property hits the market, you want to know:

1. Your adjusted cost basis and estimated gain, including depreciation taken over the years.
2. Whether you're exchanging, cashing out, or splitting, so your CPA and intermediary are lined up before escrow opens.
3. Your replacement target, so the 45-day clock doesn't force a bad purchase.
4. Your timeline with current tenants. Occupied versus vacant changes your buyer pool and your price, and California tenant protections affect how and when you can deliver the property vacant.
