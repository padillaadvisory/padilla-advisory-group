The move itself is hard enough. Then comes the question nobody wants to rush: what happens to Mom or Dad's house? It's the family's biggest asset, it's full of memories, and the bills for care are about to start. You're also probably the one coordinating all of it.

Here is a practical way to work through it, in the order that tends to save the most money and the most conflict.

## Start with what care will actually cost

In California, median assisted living runs somewhere around $6,000 or more per month by recent statewide estimates, and Los Angeles ranks among the more expensive markets. Memory care at premium communities can climb past $10,000 a month. Those are rough figures, and your parent's actual cost depends on the community and the level of care.

Add it up for a realistic time horizon, say three to five years, and compare it against two numbers: your parent's monthly income, and the net proceeds a sale would produce. That comparison usually tells you whether the house needs to fund the care or can simply be held.

## Confirm who has the legal authority

Before anyone calls an agent, find out who can legally sign. That might be your parent, if they are still competent. It might be you, under a durable power of attorney. It might be a successor trustee, if the home sits in a living trust. If none of those exist and your parent can no longer make decisions, you may be looking at a conservatorship, which takes time and court involvement.

This is the single biggest delay in these sales. If your parent still has capacity, getting documents signed now is far easier than later. An elder law attorney can usually confirm in one meeting what you hold and what's missing.

## Understand how the home interacts with Medi-Cal

If your parent may eventually need Medi-Cal to pay for long-term care, the house matters. A few points, in general terms:

- A primary residence is generally treated as an exempt asset for eligibility while the person lives there or intends to return, though details depend on the circumstances.
- California's Medi-Cal asset limit was suspended for two years and is scheduled to apply again from January 1, 2026, at about $130,000 for an individual, so cash from a home sale can matter a great deal for eligibility.
- After death, the state can seek repayment for care received at age 55 or older. Under current law that generally reaches assets that go through probate, and there are exemptions and hardship waivers with deadlines.

Selling a home before applying changes the eligibility picture, because the proceeds become countable money. Holding it can preserve exempt status but may expose it to estate recovery later. That trade-off is exactly why you want an elder law attorney in the conversation before listing.

## Weigh the three realistic options

**Sell now.** Best when the care costs will be ongoing, the house is more than the family can manage, or the market is strong. Proceeds fund care directly. The tax picture is often favorable on a longtime owner's primary residence, but a CPA should confirm.

**Keep it and rent it.** Works if the house is in good condition and your parent can afford to wait. You become a landlord, with maintenance, insurance, tenant law, and a rental income that may count against eligibility for benefits.

**Hold it vacant.** Often the costliest choice, because insurance on an empty house can be limited or expensive, and a vacant home needs regular checks, utilities, and upkeep. If you hold it, set a firm review date.

## Get the house ready without doing it all at once

If a sale is the answer, a staged approach reduces the strain. Start with the paperwork and valuables, then the rooms that matter most to family, then the rest. In many cases, selling as-is to a buyer who will do the work is a perfectly reasonable choice, and a local agent can tell you what condition-based discount to expect compared to a light cleanup and repair.

**The takeaway:** don't let the house decision drive the care decision, and don't let the care decision happen without the house in view. Know the monthly cost, confirm who has legal authority, get elder law advice on Medi-Cal, and then choose sell, rent, or hold on the numbers.
