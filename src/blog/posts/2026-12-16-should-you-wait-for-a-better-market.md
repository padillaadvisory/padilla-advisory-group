If you've decided to sell, or you're close, there's a good chance someone has told you to wait. Wait for rates to come down. Wait for spring. Wait until prices recover. It sounds prudent, and nobody wants to be the person who sold right before the market turned.

But for most longtime owners, downsizers, and families settling an estate, waiting is not a neutral choice. It has a cost, and that cost is usually bigger than people expect. Here's how to think about it with numbers instead of headlines.

## The forecasts don't support waiting for cheaper money

The most common reason sellers hold off is the hope that lower mortgage rates will bring more buyers and higher prices. The professional forecasts don't back that up right now.

In its October 2026 outlook, Fannie Mae raised its 2027 forecast for the average 30-year fixed rate to **7.5%**, up from 6.7% just a month earlier, and projected rates to stay at that level through all four quarters of 2027. The same forecast trimmed expected 2027 existing-home sales to about 4.06 million and national home price growth to just **0.6%** for the year.

Forecasts change, and nobody can call the market. But if you're waiting for a rate-driven rebound, you're betting against the people whose job it is to model this. Even in the optimistic case, a fraction of a percent of appreciation can be wiped out quickly by what it costs you to hold the house in the meantime.

## Waiting has a monthly price tag

Every month you hold a house you've already decided to leave, you keep paying for it. For a longtime owner that usually includes:

- Property taxes and homeowners insurance (and California insurance premiums have been climbing)
- Utilities, gardening, pool service, and routine maintenance
- Repairs that pop up on an older house the longer it sits
- For an inherited or vacant home: vacancy coverage, security, and the time and travel of whoever is managing it

Add those up for a year. For many Los Angeles homes it's easily tens of thousands of dollars. Then compare that number to what a realistic price gain would need to be just to break even. In a flat market, the math rarely favors waiting.

There's also a personal cost that doesn't show up in a spreadsheet. If you're downsizing, waiting means another year in a house that may no longer fit how you live. If you're an adult child or executor, it means another year of carrying a property, coordinating siblings, and keeping an estate open.

## The bigger risk isn't timing. It's pricing.

Here's where sellers actually lose money: not by listing in the "wrong" month, but by listing at the wrong price and chasing the market down.

A Realtor.com analysis published in June 2026 found that homes that sold around their fourth week on the market closed about **1.8 percentage points above** asking, while homes that lingered for 18 weeks closed about **1.3 percentage points below** asking. On a $1.5 million home, that three-point swing is roughly **$45,000**.

The pattern is familiar to anyone who has watched listings. A house that comes on too high gets toured by fewer buyers, picks up days on market, and starts to look like something is wrong with it. Then come the price cuts, and each one tells buyers they can wait you out. Realtor.com's senior economist put it bluntly: "The pandemic gave sellers a free pass on pricing and that pass has expired."

The first few weeks are when your home gets the most attention it will ever get. Pricing it to the market you actually have, not the one you hope for, is what protects your number.

## What "pricing right the first time" actually means

Pricing well isn't the same as pricing low. It means landing the list price where the most qualified buyers see it as fair, so they compete instead of negotiate. In practice:

1. **Price off recent closed sales, not active listings.** Active listings show what sellers want. Closed sales show what buyers paid.
2. **Adjust honestly for condition.** A home with original kitchens and baths competes with renovated comps on price, not on memories.
3. **Look at what's pending right now.** Pending sales are the freshest read on where buyers are.
4. **Decide your floor before you list.** Knowing your walk-away number in advance keeps you from reacting emotionally in week six.

This is the part of the job where an agent earns their fee, and it's where we spend the most time with clients at Padilla Advisory Group: building the pricing case from data before the sign goes up.

## When waiting does make sense

Sometimes holding off is the right call. If you're within a few months of a tax milestone your CPA has flagged, if a probate court step or divorce settlement isn't finalized, if the home needs a specific repair to be financeable, or if your next living situation isn't lined up, timing can matter. Those are concrete reasons with concrete end dates.

"The market might be better next year" isn't one of them.

## The takeaway

You can't control rates or the headlines. You can control your carrying costs, your preparation, and your list price. For most sellers facing a life transition, the better question isn't "Is this the perfect market?" It's "What does it cost me to wait, and am I priced to sell in the first four weeks?" Answer those two honestly and the timing decision usually makes itself.
